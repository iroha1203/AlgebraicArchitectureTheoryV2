\chapter*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Introduction}

Software designs keep changing. We replace the representation of data, factor
processing out into shared components, and split one system into several
services. In every case the question is what is preserved across the change.
Is it the value that is read, the action of an operation, or a law that held
on each part? The answer depends on the choice of what to read as structure.

This question has gained weight now that AI generates changes. The speed at
which candidate changes are written, and their volume, have grown by orders
of magnitude, and the focus of development is shifting from writing changes to
judging them.
Among the generated changes, which one preserves what? Where does the whole
break? How many choices remain? The problem that software engineering faces in
the age of AI is to judge, on solid grounds, changes that are generated faster
than a person can read them one by one. These decisions need a foundation. The whole
of it is not a task for a single paper. As that foundation, this paper
provides the objects to be judged and the theorems about them.

This paper constructs a mathematics of comparison in which the choice of what
to read and what to preserve is explicit. We start from Atoms, which are typed
primitive facts, and Laws, which are equations that objects must satisfy. We
fix a choice of vocabulary, extraction rules, object formation, equations,
operations, and locality, and call it a \emph{reading}. We define objects and
morphisms of architecture relative to a reading, and develop their geometry,
local consistency, diagnosis, transport, comparison, and reconstruction as one
system, the foundations of Algebraic Architecture Theory (AAT).

\section*{Five questions}

We introduce, through concrete situations, the questions that this paper
answers.

\textbf{When do locally valid states glue into a whole?}
Suppose that three services record the same value against different
references. Even if a correction between references can be specified for each
connection, no reference common to all three can be chosen unless the
corrections add up to zero around the cycle through the three. However
thoroughly one checks the equations inside each service, this discrepancy
stays invisible. To decide whether states that satisfy the Laws on each part
can be glued into a whole, one needs comparisons on the overlaps of the parts,
and coefficients that measure the differences (Chapter~\ref{chap:2}).

\textbf{Can the same obstruction be diagnosed under a different way of
reading?}
The same state may be read including its internal values, or only through its
published values. For a Law that reads only displayed values, merging states
with the same displayed values loses no Law value. If, however, we also
repartition the regions on which local states are placed, the cycles of
overlaps may change. To keep the same verdict while changing summaries and
partitions, one needs a map that preserves the meaning of diagnoses and a
condition under which this map is an isomorphism (Chapter~\ref{chap:3}).

\textbf{When can structure be carried along a change, and different
construction routes be made coherent?}
In a refactoring that tidies up the field names and data formats of an
order-processing API, we want to carry over to the target not only the correspondence of data
but also the operations and conditions. Moreover, several construction routes
arise from the same input: moving from the old version to the new one through
an intermediate version or directly, and splitting the whole before selecting
a part or selecting a part before splitting. Even if each conversion can be
undone, the results of the routes need not agree. We need to construct
comparisons between the routes and to decide when their discrepancies can be
removed (Chapters~\ref{chap:4}--\ref{chap:5}).

\textbf{By what information can changes that preserve a comparison be
distinguished, and how many of them remain?}
In model synchronization, a view is read from a state, and changes to the view
are written back to the state. If a change of state representation leaves the
view unchanged, reads are preserved; but applying the change and then
updating need not give the same result as updating and then applying the
change. Some changes preserve
reads and yet break updates. For protocols, we look for changes that preserve
named operations and observations and are compatible with existing adapters.
In both problems, the question is how much freedom remains among the permitted
changes, and what information is needed to pin them down
(Chapters~\ref{chap:6}--\ref{chap:7}).

\textbf{When can the whole be recovered from local descriptions?}
Suppose that the modernization of an intrabank transfer system is divided
among the account, payment, and accounting teams. Each team describes the
types, operations, and conditions in its own scope, and the correspondence
between the system before and after the modernization. For these descriptions to determine the
overall design and a single structure-preserving morphism, one needs
compatibility conditions between the descriptions and a construction that
assembles the whole from them (Chapter~\ref{chap:8}).

The five questions need the same ingredients: the structure of objects, the
action of operations, the readouts on each part together with their overlaps, and
the equations to be satisfied. Instead of preparing a separate tool for each
problem, this paper constructs objects that carry all of these at once, and
proves the answers to the five questions as theorems about them.

\section*{Architecture relative to a reading}

We call typed primitive facts \emph{Atoms}. A finite family of Atoms together
with relations is a \emph{configuration}, and an object that also keeps state
sets and the interpretation of operations is an \emph{architecture object}.
The equations that objects must satisfy are specified as \emph{Laws}. To read
information part by part, we choose local contexts, their covers, and
coefficients that measure differences. The totality of these choices is a
\emph{reading}. Relativizing architecture to a reading means making this
choice explicit together with the objects.

Chapter~\ref{chap:1} constructs three stages from this input. From an
extracted finite family of Atoms we form a base object and generate a core,
which carries the smallest family of objects closed under the permitted
operations (Theorem~\ref{thm:1.18}). From contexts and covers we construct a
site, and from restriction maps compatible with coordinates and structural
relations we construct a presheaf of rings and its sheafification, which together
give a geometry (\S\S\ref{sec:1.5}--\ref{sec:1.6}). We then define objects and
morphisms at each of the stages of extraction, core, and geometry, and
construct the tower of projections that connects them
(Theorem~\ref{thm:1.31}). We call a core enriched with covers, overlaps, and
coefficients a \emph{full geometry}; the later chapters carry out comparison
and reconstruction on these objects.

Individual problems become special cases in the theory of these common
objects. The
\emph{Rising Sea} of the title comes from a metaphor with which Grothendieck
described his way of doing mathematics. A problem is treated not as a hard
shell to be cracked with a chisel, but as something that opens by itself when
immersed in a sea whose level rises quietly
(\cite[\href{https://webusers.imj-prg.fr/~leila.schneps/grothendieckcircle/RetSbis.pdf}{Part~III, note~122, ``La mer qui monte\ldots''}]{ReS};
the English phrase \emph{rising sea} follows the translation in
\cite{McLarty03}). This paper applies this approach, raising the level
of the theory so that it envelops individual problems, to the comparison of
architectures. Indeed, gluing in Chapter~\ref{chap:2}, diagnosis in
Chapter~\ref{chap:3}, transport and base change in
Chapters~\ref{chap:4}--\ref{chap:5}, normalization and the classification of
changes in Chapters~\ref{chap:6}--\ref{chap:7}, and reconstruction in
Chapter~\ref{chap:8} all begin with constructions from the same input, and
later chapters use the outputs of earlier chapters as their inputs.

Below we describe, for each group of results, its input, the new objects and
maps it constructs, the main conditions under which it holds, and what it
passes on to later chapters.

\textbf{Foundational geometry and local consistency (Part~I,
Chapters~\ref{chap:1}--\ref{chap:2}).}
The input is a finite family of Atoms and a reading. The family of objects
generated in Chapter~\ref{chap:1} equals the family of all objects reachable by
finite sequences of operations (Theorem~\ref{thm:1.18}).

Chapter~\ref{chap:2} builds an ideal from symbolic equations and shows that,
under an algebraic presentation of the evaluation data and a localization
condition, the simultaneous vanishing of the residuals coincides with
factorization through the subspace on which the Laws hold
(Theorem~\ref{thm:2.9}). The differences of local states on overlaps define a
cocycle of degree one, and changing the local states adds a coboundary. When the
corrections act so that the local states form torsors, and the actual states
form a sheaf, the vanishing
of the obstruction class is equivalent to the existence of a global state
(Theorem~\ref{thm:2.22}). The semantics of repair and the computation with
equations are connected by a comparison isomorphism between two coefficients,
one built from a presentation by repair words and the other from a quotient by
the equations; this gives the correspondence of specified obstruction classes
and a condition for the existence of a global repair
(Theorem~\ref{thm:2.49}, Corollary~\ref{cor:2.50}).

The chapter also computes,
on an actual cover of an Atom family with points and generators, an
integer-coefficient obstruction class from local states and affine
transitions (\S\ref{sec:2.8}, Proposition~\ref{prop:2.41}), and passes it to
Chapter~\ref{chap:3} as input.

\textbf{Diagnostic invariance (Part~II, Chapter~\ref{chap:3}).}
As the quotient that identifies sources on which all Law values agree, we
construct the coarsest resolution that preserves the evaluation of Laws. This
quotient factors uniquely through every resolution that preserves the
evaluation (Theorem~\ref{thm:3.4}). From the distinct Law values that appear
on the cells we form coefficients and generate a diagnostic complex, and from
the comparisons of covers, coefficients, and resolutions we build a cochain map
that commutes with the differentials (Proposition~\ref{prop:3.14}). Under
Condition~C on the comparison of covers and coefficients, this map induces an
isomorphism on first cohomology (Theorem~\ref{thm:3.17}). Invariance for all
families of Laws for which both resolutions are adequate is equivalent to the
vanishing of the kernels and cokernels for all nonempty subsets of values, and
can be decided from a finite presentation (Theorem~\ref{thm:3.22},
Proposition~\ref{prop:3.23}). There are examples in which fixed local
observations agree but the verdicts differ, so local incidence information
alone cannot decide invariance (Proposition~\ref{prop:3.28}).

Moreover, we map
the integer-coefficient obstruction class of Chapter~\ref{chap:2} to a
diagnostic class of Law values (Theorem~\ref{thm:3.37}), and, under conditions
on the identification of relation components and on common representatives,
we make rational corrections integral and thereby reflect vanishing
(Theorem~\ref{thm:3.40}). For the refinement from three charts to four charts
built from the same Atom input, the comparison square carries the verdict,
zero or nonzero, in both directions (Theorems~\ref{thm:3.43}
and~\ref{thm:3.44}).

\textbf{Transport and comparison of construction routes (Parts~II--III,
Chapters~\ref{chap:4}--\ref{chap:5}).}
Along an exact change, which gives a bijective reindexing of the extracted
Atoms, we construct the core after the change. The canonical morphism to this core has the
universal property that every further change factors through it uniquely
(Theorem~\ref{thm:4.5}). Transport to geometry (Theorem~\ref{thm:4.11}), coherence
with units and composition, and compatibility with projections
(Theorems~\ref{thm:4.15} and~\ref{thm:4.16}) follow from this uniqueness. The
difference between a specified comparison and the canonical comparison is
expressed as an automorphism. For finite comparison diagrams, the vanishing of
this discrepancy is equivalent to the existence of an edge reselection that
makes all faces commute at once (Theorem~\ref{thm:4.23}). At both levels, core
and geometry, lifts and kernel changes for the same choice correspond to global
coherence (Theorem~\ref{thm:4.34}).

Chapter~\ref{chap:5} constructs, as a fiber
product, the original input corresponding to a scope chosen in the target,
and pulls back the structure of objects, operations, and Laws. Along an exact
base morphism, a cartesian lift to any target core exists
(Theorem~\ref{thm:5.6}), and transport and pullback form an adjoint
equivalence (Proposition~\ref{prop:5.8}). For pointed squares generated from
finite codes, the Beck--Chevalley comparison built from the units and counits
of these adjunctions is invertible and gives the exchange of the two routes
(Theorem~\ref{thm:5.11}). This correspondence preserves and reflects diagnoses
and orbits of edge reselections (Theorem~\ref{thm:5.22}). For a refinement
that increases the extracted content, reflection of extraction on the realized
locus is necessary and sufficient for backward transport
(Theorem~\ref{thm:5.25}).

At the end of the chapter, we generate the two
routes of full geometries, including covers and local data, from the same
finite input, and construct the generated comparison $\beta=E\alpha$ by
composing the canonical comparison $\alpha$ with a normalization $E$ chosen
according to the diagnosis (\S\ref{sec:5.10}).

\textbf{Normalization and classification of changes (Parts~III--IV,
Chapters~\ref{chap:6}--\ref{chap:7}).}
The map that selects one representation for each configuration is idempotent,
and under conditions ensuring that operations, residuals, invariants, and
signatures are preserved, it lifts to an idempotent morphism of full geometries
(Theorems~\ref{thm:6.5} and~\ref{thm:6.14}). A generated comparison factors
into an invertible comparison and an idempotent normalization factor, and it
is invertible if and only if the normalization factor is the identity
(Theorem~\ref{thm:6.16}). Even when the factor is not the identity, the same
comparison morphism gives an isomorphism between images in the idempotent
completion, compatible with the projection to the core
(Theorem~\ref{thm:6.12}). Normalized objects and morphisms form a category,
and we obtain a full normalization functor (Theorem~\ref{thm:6.21}), the
interchange of forming the comparison category and idempotent completion
(Theorem~\ref{thm:6.27}), and compatibility with the three-level projections
(Proposition~\ref{prop:6.28}).

Chapter~\ref{chap:7} constructs the group of
endpoint changes that preserve a single comparison
(Proposition~\ref{prop:7.3}), and determines, from the projections to the endpoints and their kernels,
whether a given change on one side admits a following change on the other,
and how much freedom remains in choosing it (Theorem~\ref{thm:7.4}). Compatibility can be determined by observations alone
if and only if the kernel of the observation homomorphism is contained in the
comparison-preserving group (Theorem~\ref{thm:7.9}). For canonical
normalizations, we construct a section of the restriction homomorphism from a
common presentation of the accompanying data, and classify all compatible
lifts as a torsor under the kernel (\S\S\ref{sec:7.4}--\ref{sec:7.6},
Theorems~\ref{thm:7.19} and~\ref{thm:7.21}). Even when compatible lifts can be
constructed, the compatibility of a given change cannot always be determined by
the normalized information alone (\S\S\ref{sec:7.4}--\ref{sec:7.5}). For
systems of operations that keep hidden states, the state changes that preserve
operations are described as families of permutations indexed by connected
components, and we obtain a split short exact sequence onto the visible group
(Theorems~\ref{thm:7.24} and~\ref{thm:7.25}).

\textbf{Presentation and local reconstruction (Part~IV,
Chapter~\ref{chap:8}).}
Even when a finite presentation recovers the meaning of an object, morphisms
between fixed presentations need not represent the whole semantic Hom-set;
over finitely many Atoms, normalization of presentations closes this gap
(\S\ref{sec:8.1}). When three conditions hold, namely separation of
morphisms by typed readout items, assembly of morphisms from compatible
correspondence tables, and assembly of objects from local descriptions, the
readout functor gives an equivalence of categories (Theorem~\ref{thm:8.12}). In the main theorem we prove that the
primitive data of types, operations, Laws, local geometry, and realization
satisfy these three conditions, and show that the category of full geometries
and all their structure-preserving morphisms is equivalent to the category of
local models given by primitive data and compatibility conditions defined
independently (Theorem~\ref{thm:8.18}). This equivalence recovers evaluation,
composition, and isomorphisms (Corollary~\ref{cor:8.19}), and applies to
design descriptions divided among teams, such as the modernization of an
intrabank transfer system (\S\ref{sec:8.6}).

The same principle applies to
lenses and protocols (Corollary~\ref{cor:8.25}): from finite tables of
reference fibers or of generating edges, general morphisms extend uniquely by
the laws of the operations (Propositions~\ref{prop:8.26}
and~\ref{prop:8.28}). For finite models, we distinguish the information
sufficient for reconstruction, the checking procedure, and the computational
cost (\S\ref{sec:8.8}), and, as a supplement, we discuss the limits of
presentation and reconstruction when infinite objects are allowed
(\S\ref{sec:8.9}).

\section*{Correspondence with the semantics of CS}

Each application to a problem in computer science (CS) follows a single
pattern of argument.
From an independently defined problem and its semantics we construct an
input for AAT, prove a correspondence proposition, apply a common theorem, and bring
the consequence back to the original problem. Each correspondence proposition
states in which direction it holds: equivalence, implication, or preservation.

For model synchronization, we define state sets, views, reads, updates, and
their laws independently (\S\ref{sec:1.8}). When Atoms, operations, and Laws
are constructed from this semantics, the constructed Laws hold if and only if
the original laws hold (Proposition~\ref{prop:1.41}), and typed morphisms
are in one-to-one correspondence with the original semantics-preserving
morphisms
(Proposition~\ref{prop:1.43}). For protocols, we define control points,
states, named operations, and observations independently (\S\ref{sec:1.9}).
Protocols are encoded in the same framework through realization from finite
operation tables (Proposition~\ref{prop:1.37}) and recovery of
semantics-preserving morphisms from vertex maps
(Proposition~\ref{prop:1.38}).

Applied to these correspondences, the common theorems yield the following
consequences: simultaneous preservation of reads and updates, and transport
of named operations and adapters by commutative diagrams
(\S\S\ref{sec:4.10}--\ref{sec:4.11}), together with a correspondence
between the semantics and typed constructions that preserves comparisons
(Theorem~\ref{thm:4.41}); compatibility of restriction to a chosen scope
with splitting (Propositions~\ref{prop:5.34} and~\ref{prop:5.36}); the
operations that remain in the image of a normalization (\S\ref{sec:6.8});
and the classification and number of changes that preserve operations
(\S\S\ref{sec:7.7}--\ref{sec:7.8}).

For example, for a product lens, of the four
candidate changes that preserve the displayed shipping address and the unset
value, only two also preserve updates. In the example of workers with
internal states, of the sixteen candidates that preserve the correspondence
of control points, only four preserve the handoff of contexts. These
numbers show how much freedom of change remains once each operation ties the
correspondences between states together. Finally, reconstruction from finite
tables supports the division of descriptions and their checking
(\S\ref{sec:8.7}).

\section*{Organization of the paper}

The main body consists of four parts and eight chapters. Part~I
(Chapters~\ref{chap:1}--\ref{chap:2}) constructs the objects and addresses
local consistency. Part~II (Chapters~\ref{chap:3}--\ref{chap:4}) treats
changes of reading and transport. Part~III
(Chapters~\ref{chap:5}--\ref{chap:6}) compares construction routes and
studies normalization. Part~IV (Chapters~\ref{chap:7}--\ref{chap:8})
classifies changes and carries out recovery from presentations. The
Preliminaries, which precede the main body, fix the assumptions shared by all
chapters, such as the sizes of sets and categories, the order of maps and
composition, and the conventions for coefficients and notation. Definitions
specific to AAT are introduced from Chapter~\ref{chap:1} on. For general
notions such as sites, sheaves, cohomology, and idempotent completion, the
needed content and the original sources are given in the chapters that use
them.

Readers mainly interested in diagnosis may concentrate on
Chapters~\ref{chap:1}--\ref{chap:3}; for transport and generated comparisons,
on Chapters~\ref{chap:1}, \ref{chap:2}, and~\ref{chap:4}--\ref{chap:6}; and
for the classification of changes and reconstruction, on
Chapters~\ref{chap:6}--\ref{chap:8}.

Related Work examines, for each main result, the closely related original
literature and compares objects, assumptions, admissible morphisms, and
conclusions.
Appendix~\ref{app:A} lists the Lean declarations that correspond to the
definitions, constructions, and results of the text, together with their
objects, assumptions, conclusions, and the fixed version of the sources.
Appendix~\ref{app:B} describes where the sources of the proofs are published
and how to check them by building.
